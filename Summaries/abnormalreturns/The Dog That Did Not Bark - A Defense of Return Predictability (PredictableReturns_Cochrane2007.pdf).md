# The Dog That Did Not Bark: A Defense of Return Predictability

John H. Cochrane. *The Review of Financial Studies* 21(4), 2008, 1533–1575; advance publication September 22, 2007. DOI: 10.1093/rfs/hhm046. Source: `Finance/PredictableReturns_Cochrane2007.pdf`, 43 pages. Journal page 1533 is PDF page 1. The local filename dates the advance-publication version; the supplied article has the 2008 journal pagination. This note covers the complete argument, simulations, robustness discussion, and reported tables.

## Main claim and innovative component

The central claim is that evidence about expected returns cannot be evaluated independently of evidence about expected cash flows. A market price-dividend ratio varies because investors anticipate different future dividends, different required returns, or a nonvanishing terminal valuation component. If returns are assumed unpredictable and explosive valuation behavior is excluded, changing dividend yields must forecast dividend growth. Yet aggregate dividend yields in the studied data have almost no ability to forecast subsequent dividend growth, including over long horizons. That missing forecast is the paper's “dog that did not bark.”

Cochrane does not discover that a standard one-year return regression has overwhelming conventional significance. He reproduces the familiar weak result: under a coherent constant-expected-return model, one-year return coefficients as large as the observed one occur about 20% of the time. The innovation is to evaluate additional implications of that same null. Dividend-growth forecasts, or an economically equivalent long-run return coefficient, are much harder for the null to reconcile with the data. Depending on the persistence assumptions, their simulated significance levels are around 1–2%, rather than around 20%.

The paper also reconciles two apparently contrary observations. Ordinary five-year return regressions often add little inferential power, yet appropriately constructed very-long-run coefficients can be more informative. And dividend-yield forecasts can perform poorly against the historical mean out of sample even when all valuation-ratio variation is generated by changing expected returns. The first distinction concerns the geometry of a joint statistical test; the second concerns parameter estimation and the difference between an economic hypothesis and a usable real-time forecast.

This is an argument about identification and inference in a simple aggregate model. It does not establish a frictionless trading opportunity, select an optimal forecasting specification, or distinguish rational variation in required compensation from behavioral variation in discount rates. Its economic conclusion is about what historical valuation movements appear to represent.

## Data and baseline evidence

The analysis uses annual CRSP value-weighted market data through 2004. Table 1 describes annual data over 1926–2004; the core forecasting-system estimates use 1927–2004 outcomes and lagged yields. Returns and dividend growth are deflated by the CPI for the real-return specification. Excess returns subtract the real return on three-month Treasury bills. Lowercase letters denote logarithms.

Table 1 first shows ordinary level regressions. Real returns on lagged dividend-price have slope 3.39, statistic 2.28, and $R^2=5.8\%$. Excess returns have slope 3.83, statistic 2.61, and $R^2=7.4\%$. The standard deviations of fitted expected returns are approximately 4.9 and 5.6 percentage points, respectively. These are economically large compared with a sample average equity premium around 7.7%, even though monthly-style intuition might regard a single-digit $R^2$ as small.

Real dividend growth, by contrast, has a level-regression coefficient of only 0.07 and a statistic of 0.06. In logarithms, the real-return slope is 0.097 and the dividend-growth slope 0.008. The latter is slightly positive, the opposite of what a cash-flow interpretation of valuation ratios requires: a high dividend yield means a low price and should signal weak future dividend growth if changing cash-flow expectations explain the low price.

Dividends are recovered from CRSP total returns and returns excluding distributions. If $R_{t+1}$ and $R^x_{t+1}$ denote gross total and ex-distribution returns, then

$$
\frac{D_{t+1}}{P_{t+1}}=\frac{R_{t+1}}{R^x_{t+1}}-1,
$$

and dividend growth follows from the ratio of successive yields multiplied by the ex-distribution price return. This convention effectively reinvests distributions paid earlier in the year at the market return through year-end. It preserves the end-of-year return identity but contributes to measured dividend-growth volatility. Changing the distribution-accumulation convention would require revisiting that identity and the simulation calibration.

## The joint predictive model

Let $x_t=d_t-p_t$ denote log dividend yield. The paper estimates

$$
r_{t+1}=a_r+b_r x_t+\varepsilon^r_{t+1},
$$

$$
\Delta d_{t+1}=a_d+b_d x_t+\varepsilon^d_{t+1},
$$

$$
x_{t+1}=a_x+\phi x_t+\varepsilon^x_{t+1}.
$$

The model is intentionally first order and uses only dividend yield as a predictor. Additional lags and returns or dividend growth add little in the simple specification examined. It is not a claim that all information relevant to expected returns is contained in dividend yield.

The loglinearized return identity, suppressing constants and means, is

$$
r_{t+1}=x_t-\rho x_{t+1}+\Delta d_{t+1},
$$

where $\rho=\overline{P/D}/(1+\overline{P/D})$ is the linearization coefficient, not the predictor's autoregressive coefficient. The distinction between $\rho$ and $\phi$ is essential. In the main calibration, $\rho$ is about 0.9638 and estimated persistence $\phi$ is about 0.941.

Projecting the identity onto current yield gives

$$
b_r=1-\rho\phi+b_d,
$$

while innovations obey

$$
\varepsilon^r_{t+1}=\varepsilon^d_{t+1}-\rho\varepsilon^x_{t+1}.
$$

Thus only two of the three predictive equations are independent. An investigator cannot freely set both $b_r$ and $b_d$ to zero while retaining ordinary mean-reverting yields. Doing so would require $\rho\phi=1$, a boundary associated with a terminal valuation component rather than a standard convergent present-value relation.

For real returns, inflation is subtracted from both return and dividend-growth sides. For excess returns, the corresponding cash-flow variable is dividend growth less the risk-free return. It is therefore misleading to compare an excess-return decomposition with an unadjusted dividend-growth regression without also including the interest-rate term. Cochrane incorporates that term into the cash-flow side of the excess-return calculations.

## Constructing a coherent null

Under the null that returns are unpredictable, impose $b_r=0$. For any specified $\phi$, the accounting restriction forces

$$
b_d=\rho\phi-1.
$$

At $\phi=0.941$, this is approximately $-0.0931$; at $\phi=0.99$, it is approximately $-0.046$. The null therefore predicts a substantial negative relation between dividend yield and subsequent dividend growth. It cannot claim that neither variable is predictable while leaving the observed yield variation unexplained.

The simulation evolves dividend yield and dividend growth, then constructs returns from the identity. Innovations are Gaussian, with covariance taken from the estimated dividend-growth and dividend-yield residuals. For stationary persistence, the initial demeaned yield is drawn from

$$
x_0\sim N\left(0,\frac{\sigma_x^2}{1-\phi^2}\right),
$$

where $\sigma_x^2$ here denotes the innovation variance, not the unconditional yield variance. For $\phi\geq1$, the initial value is set to zero. Each null is evaluated using 50,000 artificial histories of the historical sample length, and all three predictive regressions are reestimated in each history.

Table 2's residual standard deviations are approximately 19.66% for returns, 14.07% for dividend growth, and 15.30% for yield innovations. Return and yield innovations correlate at about $-70\%$; dividend-growth and yield innovations correlate only about 7.5%. Return and dividend-growth innovations correlate about 66%. Those relationships are tightly linked by the identity and are not freely adjustable nuisance assumptions.

The loglinearization is approximate. Directly estimated coefficients differ from the values implied by the other two equations by roughly 0.005. Cochrane therefore reports ranges over alternative choices of which coefficient is inferred from the identity. A replication should retain this distinction rather than mixing independently estimated coefficients and then expecting the decomposition to sum exactly to one.

## The first simulation result: missing dividend predictability

At persistence 0.941, simulated one-year real-return slopes exceed the observed slope 22.3% of the time. Corresponding return statistics exceed their observed values about 10.3% of the time. These results reproduce the small-sample critique: a conventional-looking statistic near two is not compelling once persistent predictors and correlated innovations are handled correctly.

The dividend-growth result is very different. A slope as high as the observed approximately zero value occurs only 1.77% of the time under the null, and a dividend-growth statistic as high as observed occurs 1.67% of the time. For excess returns, the corresponding cash-flow-side frequencies are 1.11% and 0.87%. The constant-return null generally produces clearly negative dividend-growth coefficients; it rarely produces the observed absence of that relation.

This does not amount to inferring predictability from ignorance. The null makes a positive, testable prediction about cash flows. The data contradict that prediction. Nor does the inference change the point estimate of the return regression: the observed return slope already implies economically meaningful predictability. It changes the assessment of how plausibly the whole set of observed coefficients could arise in a world with constant expected returns.

The use of the joint model also prevents an incoherent defense of the null. Taking the return coefficient's weak significance as support for constant returns while simultaneously taking dividend growth's insignificant coefficient as support for unpredictable dividends would combine two conclusions that cannot both hold under the maintained present-value model and persistence restriction.

## Long-run coefficients and valuation variance

Forward iteration, with a vanishing terminal component, gives

$$
x_t=E_t\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\rho^{j-1}r_{t+j}-E_t\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\rho^{j-1}\Delta d_{t+j}.
$$

Within the first-order system, the coefficients of those long-run outcomes on current yield are

$$
b_r^{LR}=\frac{b_r}{1-\rho\phi},\qquad b_d^{LR}=\frac{b_d}{1-\rho\phi},
$$

and they satisfy

$$
b_r^{LR}-b_d^{LR}=1.
$$

Multiplying the present-value identity by demeaned yield and taking expectations shows that $b_r^{LR}$ and $-b_d^{LR}$ are covariance contributions to yield variance. They are not orthogonal variance shares, so one can exceed 100% while the other is negative. Calling an estimate above one impossible would misunderstand the decomposition.

Table 4 gives a long-run real-return coefficient of 1.09 with standard error 0.44, and a dividend-growth coefficient of 0.09 with the same standard error when the identity is imposed consistently. The excess-return coefficient is 1.23 with standard error 0.47. These point estimates say that expected-return variation accounts for essentially all observed yield variation, while dividend-growth forecasts contribute little or move in the wrong direction.

The real-return simulated significance range is approximately 1.39–1.83%, depending on the coefficient-imputation convention; excess-return values in the table are 0.47–0.69%. Using directly estimated persistence instead produces a real-return coefficient near 1.04, with standard error 0.42. The numerical differences matter for exact reproduction but do not change the basic decomposition.

Long-run return and dividend tests are two expressions of the same restriction. Testing $b_r^{LR}=0$ is equivalent to testing $b_d^{LR}=-1$. This removes the need to select whichever short-run regression happens to look most favorable. The long-run parameter has a direct economic interpretation: what portion of valuation-ratio movements corresponds to changing return expectations?

## Why the joint test is informative

Across simulated samples, estimated $b_r$ and estimated $\phi$ are strongly negatively correlated. A sample that accidentally generates a large return-prediction slope often also generates a low persistence estimate. The long-run coefficient requires both a sufficiently large slope and sufficiently persistent expected-return variation for the effects to accumulate. A large numerator combined with a much larger denominator need not imply a large long-run effect.

Geometrically, a test based only on $b_r$ draws a vertical boundary in the plane of return slope and yield persistence. A long-run coefficient draws a downward-sloping boundary. Many artificial samples with large one-year slopes lie below that diagonal because their yields mean-revert rapidly and their dividend-growth forecasts are substantial. Economically, those samples are closer to a cash-flow-driven valuation world than the actual data, even though their one-year slope alone looks more extreme.

The negative innovation correlation is a substantive feature of the data. With return volatility about 20% and yield-innovation volatility about 15%, making return and yield shocks uncorrelated would force dividend-growth volatility to approximately

$$
\sqrt{0.20^2+0.96^2\times0.15^2}\approx0.25,
$$

far above the observed 14%. It would also imply a much larger positive relationship between dividend-growth and yield innovations. Price movements dominate observed return variation, so a negative return-yield innovation relation follows naturally from the accounting identity and relative volatilities.

Cochrane discusses power through this joint-distribution geometry. He does not provide a comprehensive alternative-by-alternative size-adjusted power table. Lower simulated tail probabilities at the observed estimate are not, by definition, a formal power calculation. The paper explicitly distinguishes those concepts, while arguing that the plotted rejection regions explain why the joint tests detect economically relevant alternatives more effectively.

## Persistence sensitivity and its economic cost

The stronger inference relies on an upper bound for $\phi$. If persistence could be arbitrarily explosive, sufficiently large values could reproduce a large long-run statistic even with zero return predictability. Cochrane argues that such nulls are economically incoherent. Values above $1/\rho$, about 1.04, make the discounted terminal valuation term explode. At the exact boundary, a rational-bubble interpretation becomes possible, but that is a different maintained economic model.

A unit root at $\phi=1$ does not violate the loglinear present-value convergence condition, but it produces infinite unconditional yield variance and eventually moves the process outside the region where the linearization is credible. The observed persistence estimate alone does not reject a unit root strongly: simulations at a true unit root often generate estimates near 0.941. The paper therefore supplements short-sample inference with arguments about implausibly large valuation dispersion over centuries.

Table 5 makes the sensitivity transparent. For real returns, the dividend-growth tail probability rises from 1.6% at persistence 0.941 to 4.9% at 0.98, 6.3% at 0.99, and 8.7% at 1.00. The long-run real-return ranges similarly rise to about 5.9–7.4% at 0.99 and 8.1–10% at one. Thus it would overstate the article to claim a uniformly below-five-percent result over every nonexplosive persistence value.

Excess-return evidence is stronger. Dividend-side frequencies are 2.7% at 0.98, 3.6% at 0.99, and 4.4% at one. Long-run frequencies at one are about 3.7–4.8%. Meanwhile the one-year return-slope tail probability remains around 16–22% across these specifications. The joint test remains substantially more informative even when the conventional significance threshold changes.

The implied half-life increases from 11.4 years at 0.941 to 34.3 years at 0.98 and 69 years at 0.99. The unconditional standard deviation of log yield rises from 0.45 to 0.77 and 1.09. The table's latter value differs slightly from a nearby prose approximation; the table is the numerical reference here. These are economic consequences of the nuisance parameter, not merely alternative standard-error settings.

As an integrated calculation, the paper draws persistence from its concentrated unconditional likelihood, which assigns zero probability at the stationarity boundary. The final row of Table 5 gives real-return long-run tail probabilities of 1.4–1.7% and excess-return values of 0.6–0.8%. This combines persistence uncertainty under a particular likelihood-based weighting; it is not a worst-case confidence statement valid for every admissible persistence.

## Reconciling the long-horizon literature

A finite-horizon coefficient implied by the model is

$$
b_r^{(k)}=b_r\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(\rho\phi)^j=b_r\frac{1-(\rho\phi)^k}{1-\rho\phi}.
$$

At two or five years, this combination does not put enough weight on persistence to exploit the negative correlation with the estimated return slope. Table 6 reports approximately 22% tail probability at one year, 28–29% for directly estimated five-year coefficients, and 16–17% for implied five-year coefficients at persistence 0.94. Ordinary five-year regressions therefore do not deliver the large improvement sometimes attributed to “long horizons.”

At twenty years, the weighted implied coefficient's probability falls to 4.1%, and at the infinite horizon to 1.8%. Under persistence 0.99 those become 9.8% and 7.3%, reiterating the role of the persistence bound. Weighted versus unweighted returns matter less than horizon and the distinction between directly estimated and model-implied coefficients.

This is not a recommendation to regress overlapping thirty-year realized returns on a small number of historical yields. The main statistic is computed from one-year estimates and summarizes an economically meaningful combination of them. Model-implied coefficients benefit from the first-order specification; they can be misleading if that specification misses low-frequency behavior. Direct long-horizon estimates provide a useful check but lose observations and introduce extra uncertainty.

The paper checks that concern by forecasting dividend growth directly out to roughly twenty-five years. Dividend-growth coefficients remain small or have the wrong sign, while cumulative return coefficients approach or exceed one. Summing separately estimated annual-ahead coefficients, which preserves more observations at short horizons, gives the expected negative dividend-growth sign but still little explanatory magnitude. The evidence does not reveal a hidden large cash-flow response merely delayed by dividend smoothing.

## Why poor out-of-sample performance does not settle the hypothesis

The second simulation reverses the economic null: dividend growth is unpredictable and all yield variation represents changing expected returns. Consistency requires $b_d=0$ and $b_r=1-\rho\phi$. This is about 0.10 at persistence 0.941 and about 0.05 at 0.99. Some displayed text in the supplied PDF reverses that sign, but its central coefficient identity and subsequent calibration unambiguously imply the positive expression used here.

Starting after twenty years, each simulated history compares an expanding-history dividend-yield regression forecast with an expanding historical-mean forecast. The score is the historical-mean root mean squared error minus the predictive-regression root mean squared error. Positive values favor the predictor; negative values favor the mean.

The observed score is negative, confirming the poor historical out-of-sample record. Yet 32% of simulations at persistence 0.94 and 39% at 0.99 do even worse. Only about 20% of simulated histories produce positive scores, despite predictability being the sole source of valuation variation by construction. Persistent predictors and a short effective history make slope and intercept estimates noisy enough that the simple mean frequently wins.

The result does not show that out-of-sample evaluation is useless. It is directly relevant to an investor deciding whether to deploy a forecast. It shows that this particular performance statistic cannot reliably distinguish a constant-return world from a realistically calibrated predictable-return world in this sample. A sensible investor can believe expected returns vary while using restrained allocation rules because the mapping from valuations to expected returns is poorly estimated.

## Bias, replication details, and implications

Table 7 confirms that the simulations account for finite-sample bias rather than ignoring it. Under the 0.941 constant-return null, the mean estimated return coefficient is 0.049 even though the true coefficient is zero, and mean estimated persistence is 0.886. At true persistence 0.99, those means are 0.057 and 0.926. Long-run return coefficients average 0.24 and 0.43 under the two nulls, still well below the observed coefficient near one. Dividend-growth coefficients have much less bias because their shocks are nearly uncorrelated with yield innovations.

A rough bias adjustment can lower the short-run return slope and raise persistence while leaving the long-run ratio almost unchanged. For example, a slope near 0.05 and persistence near 0.99 still produce a long-run return coefficient around one. The inference concerns the joint economic implication, not insistence that the unadjusted annual slope is the true population value.

Replication requires reconstructing annual distributions consistently with returns, estimating the real and excess systems separately, fixing the loglinearization convention, preserving the residual covariance matrix, and simulating each persistence scenario with its stated initial-condition rule. It should record which coefficient is implied from the identity and reproduce the simulated coefficient distributions before comparing tail probabilities. No random seed or complete executable implementation is supplied in the article, so small Monte Carlo differences are expected. Changes to dividend construction can create larger substantive differences.

The first-order Gaussian specification, fixed residual covariance, and stationarity arguments are meaningful limitations. Repurchases and changing payout policies may require richer forecasting variables even when the accounting identity remains valid. The paper is about aggregate market dividends, whose aggregation smooths firm-level lumpiness; it should not be transferred mechanically to individual firms with infrequent or zero payouts. Additional information can forecast both dividends and returns without violating the identity because the simple variance decomposition concerns forecasts projected on dividend yield specifically.

The economic implications extend beyond tactical market timing. If a large share of market valuation movements reflects changing required excess returns, cost-of-capital estimates should vary with investment conditions, and stock-market covariance may reflect shared discount-rate news as well as shared cash-flow news. These are implications suggested by the paper's interpretation, not separate empirical tests conducted in it. The most secure conclusion is that a coherent predictability test must ask what else would have to move if expected returns did not, then confront those additional implications with the same data.

## Additional checks on the economic interpretation

The terminal term is the precise boundary between the paper's main explanation and a bubble explanation. At a finite horizon, yield variance is divided among return forecasts, dividend-growth forecasts, and forecasts of the future valuation ratio. As the horizon increases, the last component should disappear if the discounted valuation process is stable. If it does not disappear, the inference that returns and dividends exhaust valuation variation is incomplete. Cochrane therefore treats the persistence restriction as an economic part of the hypothesis, not as a convenient statistical assumption added after examining significance.

The finite-horizon decomposition also explains why low near-term cash-flow predictability alone would be insufficient. Investors might anticipate cash flows arriving much later, while intermediate valuations carry that information forward. The direct long-horizon dividend tests address this possibility. Their failure to uncover a large negative dividend-growth coefficient strengthens the argument beyond the one-year regression, although the long-horizon confidence intervals are necessarily wide and their overlapping observations are highly dependent.

The two approximately equivalent tests cease to be identical if the observed short-run dividend coefficient differs materially from zero. A test on $b_d$ holds its raw coefficient boundary fixed, whereas a test on $b_d/(1-\rho\phi)$ changes the relevant threshold with persistence. Cochrane favors the latter when the economic question concerns the fraction of valuation variation explained by cash flows. In the actual sample, $b_d$ is close enough to zero that both approaches give similar answers; that coincidence should not be generalized to every dataset.

Finally, the present-value result does not say that dividend growth is intrinsically unpredictable from every information set. Another state variable can forecast dividends and expected returns together while leaving their difference consistent with current yield. A strong earnings-based forecast of dividend growth would therefore not automatically overturn this paper. The relevant challenge would be to show that such forecasts explain the observed valuation ratio and materially change the joint cash-flow/discount-rate decomposition under a specified, reproducible expanded model.
